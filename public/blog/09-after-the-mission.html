After the Mission: Summaries, AAR and the Final Report

By John Cornelison, KE7KDQ · November 2, 2026 · about a 6-minute read

RangerTrak is a free, open-source radio log and map for the scribe at a CERT, ACS/ARES, SAR or wildland-fire command post. Log who, where, when and what off the radio; it maps them. It works offline once set up, and prints the Form 309 Communications Log, ICS-213 and ICS-214. I built it. It’s AGPL and will stay free. Try it at rangertrak.org, no account or install needed.

The last team checks in, the IC says “stand down,” and the radio goes quiet. For most of the people in the room the mission is over. For the scribe it isn’t quite. There’s the debrief, the paperwork for the coordinator, and a laptop full of names and phone numbers that has to go home in a sensible state.

On paper, the end of a mission is a box. The 309s go in it, the 214s, the sign-in sheet, and whatever got written on the whiteboard at the hot wash. It works, and a box doesn’t need charging. What it doesn’t do is add anything up. Somebody reads back through the log to find the three calls that never got an answer, and the “we should fix that” remarks from hour two are mostly gone by hour nine.

Here’s what RangerTrak does at the end, in the order I’d do it.

The debrief: the whole mission in one summary

Part 8’s Current summary works for the whole mission too. On the Radio Log or the AAR notes page, press it and choose Whole log instead of this operational period. You get how many messages came in each hour, which teams, stations and operators they came from, and a Needs attention list: the entries still flagged, with their times and message numbers. That list is where a hot wash should start.

Print it for the debrief, and Export it as a spreadsheet if your coordinator wants the numbers. Both are stamped with the mission, the period covered and when they were made. Nothing about the summary is saved on the device, so print it before you clear anything.

AAR notes, turned into an improvement plan

During the mission, the AAR/214 button at the top of every page catches “we should fix that” the moment someone says it. One line, marked as about this incident or about RangerTrak, and you’re back on the page you were on. By the end there’s a list, written at the time, not reconstructed from memory.

At the debrief, open the AAR notes page and give each note an area, a recommendation and an owner. Then Print: the incident notes come out as an improvement-plan table, the shape an after-action report already uses. Export saves every note as a spreadsheet, for whoever writes the AAR. (Since beta 2, each note can also take HSEEP’s capability element: see What You Said You’d Fix.)

Notes stay on the laptop. They aren’t sent anywhere. The one exception is up to you: a note marked about RangerTrak can come to me through Review and send, which opens Help → Contact us with the note filled in, so you can read and edit it first. That one becomes a public GitHub issue, so it’s worth that read.

The ICS-214, on FEMA’s form

The same AAR/214 box keeps your ICS-214 activity log: arrived, relieved, the repeater went down at 14:20. On the AAR notes page, Export ICS-214 fills in FEMA’s own ICS-214 form, offline. It asks whose log it is (name, ICS position, home agency and unit, kept for next time) and which lines to use: only this operational period, or all of them. The signature is left blank for you to sign by hand.

It also offers a few suggested lines from the device’s journal (more on that next), such as when the mission was backed up or the 309 printed. None of them is ticked. Tick one and it becomes an ordinary line you can edit.

One record, one stamp

RangerTrak keeps a journal of what was done to the mission’s data on this device: demos loaded, backups, restores, imports, exports, prints, anything cleared. It records events, not contents: the journal never holds what anyone said on the radio, an address or a passphrase. You’ll find it on the Log page, on its Journal tab.

Each journal entry is chained to the one before it, and the tab says whether the chain is intact. The newest entry’s short code, something like jrnl 3f9a·c21b (143), is printed at the foot of the 309, the summary and the map sheet. So the pages in the box carry the same stamp, and anyone can later check that the laptop’s journal still agrees with the paper. The mission backup carries the journal too, stamp and all.

I want to be careful about what that proves. It catches accidental damage and casual edits. It is not proof against someone determined: whoever holds the laptop holds the storage. The signed paper is still the record. The stamp just ties the paper and the laptop together.

The roster: what changed at the desk

Part 7 loaded your roster from your own spreadsheet. During a real event, people get added and corrected at the desk: a walk-up volunteer, a new phone number, a call sign that was wrong. Those changes are on this laptop only. On the Rangers page, Export changes saves just those people as a spreadsheet, with a column saying whether each was added, edited or removed. Send it to whoever keeps your master list, so the next import doesn’t bring the old details back.

That file is plain, with no passphrase, and it holds names and phone numbers. Treat it the way you’d treat the paper sign-in sheet.

Back up, then clear

Before you clear anything, on the Mission page, Back up mission. One file holds the settings, roster, places, AAR notes, 214 lines and every report. Give it a passphrase and it’s locked. A forgotten passphrase can’t be recovered, so store it the way your group stores other credentials. Put the backup wherever your group keeps mission records, not on the thumb drive that gets wiped next week.

Then, on the Radio Log page, Delete radio log entries and messages opens a What to clear box. The radio log, with its attachments, always goes. Everything else is listed with a count and is not ticked: this incident’s AAR notes, the 214 lines, and any notes about RangerTrak. You choose. There’s a Back up mission first button right there, and if the mission still looks live, you have to type its name to go ahead. There’s no undo after that, which is why the backup comes first.

The roster is separate, on purpose. It isn’t tied to one mission, so on your group’s own laptop you can leave it for next time. On a borrowed or shared laptop, use Delete all rangers on the Rangers page; the ranger photos go with it. If your roster stays on a laptop between missions, turn on Device encryption (Mission → Data safety) so a lost laptop doesn’t hand it over in the clear.

What it doesn’t do

It doesn’t send your report anywhere. There’s no server, so the summary, the 214 and the AAR spreadsheet go to your coordinator the way paper always has: printed, emailed or on a stick, by you.

It doesn’t write the after-action report. It gives whoever does the notes, the times and the numbers, collected while things happened. The judgment is still theirs.

And it isn’t an archive. A browser is a working space, not a records system. Once you’ve printed, exported and backed up, the laptop’s copy is the least important one. Keep the box. The paper is the record that survives a wiped laptop and a lost passphrase.

Try it in ten minutes

  1. Open rangertrak.org in a spare browser profile, so nothing of yours is replaced, and use Load a demo on the Entry page.
  2. Press AAR/214 and add two AAR notes and one ICS-214 line.
  3. On the AAR notes page, give one note an area, a recommendation and an owner, then Print to see the improvement-plan table.
  4. Press Export ICS-214 and open the PDF.
  5. On the Radio Log page, press Delete radio log entries and messages, look at what it offers, and press Cancel.
  6. For the guided version, take the tour: module 7, After the mission, prints the 309, a 213 and the map, and exports the 214. It uses the mission you build in modules 4 to 6.
